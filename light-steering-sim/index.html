Steering light with a second wave

An incident beam plus a wave you add. The left rail sets both. The field map shows what you would see in the plane of propagation, the scope below it shows where the energy goes far away, and the readout compares the measured exit angle θ with the law that predicts it.

What this simulation tests, in brief

The question. Glass bends light because its electrons re-radiate a wave at the light's own frequency, and the sum of that wave and the incident wave points in a new direction. Can we skip the glass and produce that second wave ourselves, so that light bends in empty space?

What is computed. One incident beam plus one added wave, both at the same frequency, summed point by point (the field map) and followed far away to see which directions carry the power (the scope). The program finds the exit angle from the computed field. It does not use the formula to find it. It then puts the result next to the formula so you can check that they agree.

Three ways to make the added wave (the selector at the top of the controls):

  1. A · Second beam in vacuum. Fringes appear where the beams cross, but the incident beam leaves at its original angle. Result: no bending.
  2. B · Hologram lit by the same laser, projecting from a distance. The hologram rebuilds exactly the wave glass would have produced: the "refracted" wave plus a wave that cancels the original. Above z = 0 the light really does leave at the Snell's-law angle for the index n₂ you choose. Two catches show on screen. The cancellation also happens below z = 0, because a wave in free space cannot switch on at a plane. And the bent light's power comes from the hologram arm, not from the incident beam. Result: the field looks refracted, but the bend has no surface and the incident beam's power is not redirected.
  3. C · Thin hologram / emitter sheet at z = 0, lit by the incident light itself. Each point on the sheet re-radiates the light that hits it with a programmed phase. This is Feynman's mechanism done artificially (a metasurface or thin phase hologram). The bend starts exactly at the sheet, and the incident power is what gets redirected. Result: true refraction or diffraction without a bulk medium.

The rule that decides the exit angle in every case: sin θₜ = sin θᵢ + (λ/2π)·dΦ/dx, where dΦ/dx is how fast the added wave's phase changes along the surface. It only works while the added wave stays phase-locked to the incident light: Δf = 0, and a path difference well inside the laser's coherence length.

1. What Feynman actually showed

In Vol. I, Ch. 31 Feynman explains the refractive index without ever saying light "slows down" inside glass. The incident wave Es shakes the electrons in a thin plate. Each electron is a driven, damped oscillator, so it oscillates at the same frequency ω as the light, with a fixed phase lag. A whole plane of such oscillators radiates a plane wave (Ch. 30, §30-7), and that wave is 90° behind the drive because the field of a sheet of charges is proportional to their velocity, not their displacement. Downstream you see the sum:

Eafter = Es + Eplate ≈ Es · (1 + i·k(n−1)Δz) ≈ Es · ei k (n−1) Δz

A small wave added in quadrature looks exactly like a phase delay, which is what a slower wave would produce. Strictly this is refraction, not diffraction, but both come from the same arithmetic: you superpose the incident wave with secondary waves of the same frequency and a definite phase, and you look at what the sum does. The Ewald–Oseen extinction theorem takes this to the limit. Inside a thick medium the dipoles' waves cancel the incident wave completely and replace it with a new wave that travels at c/n.

2. Can a wave you send in from outside do the same job?

Not by simply crossing it with another beam in empty space. Maxwell's equations in vacuum are linear, so two waves superpose and then pass through each other unchanged. Where they overlap you get interference fringes with period λ/|sin θ₁ − sin θ₂|. That redistributes energy locally but does not change either beam's direction. Far away you still find two beams, each at its original angle. Use the Free beam crossing mode to see this: the scope shows two separate peaks, and the incident peak never moves, whatever the amplitude or phase of the second beam. The only true light-on-light scattering in vacuum comes from quantum electrodynamics (Euler–Heisenberg). It has been observed only with the enormous fields of ultra-relativistic heavy-ion collisions, and is negligible for any laser.

Yes, if the added wave is built to be what the material would have radiated. The plate is just a sheet of sources locked to the incident light. Replace the electrons with any sources you control (antennas, a phased array, nano-resonators, a spatial light modulator) and the downstream field is the same. The sheet mode does exactly this. Each emitter at xj radiates

c(xj) = Einc(xj) · [ A·ei(Φ(xj) + φ₀) − κ ]

The −κ term is the extinction wave that removes the original beam. The A term is the new wave. Φ(x) is the phase profile you write across the sheet. Four conditions have to hold, and each has a slider:

  • Same frequency, phase-locked. If the added wave is off by Δf, the cross term between the two waves averages to zero over a time 1/Δf. There is then no lasting cancellation and no steering, only two independent beams. In practice the added wave has to be derived from the incident light itself, which is what a material or a passive metasurface does automatically. Try the Frequency mismatch slider.
  • A phase gradient along the sheet. A uniform added wave only delays the beam (Feynman's plate). To turn it, the phase must change across the sheet. That is the governing law below.
  • Sources on a fine enough pitch. If the emitters are more than about λ/2 apart, extra beams appear (grating lobes) at sin θm = sin θt + mλ/d.
  • Cancellation costs energy. To remove the old beam you must radiate an equal and opposite wave. The sources absorb the incident power and re-emit it in the new direction. If κ = 0, the old beam is still there and you have only added a beam. A sheet of simple point sources also radiates backward, which shows up as reflection. Huygens sheets, which pair electric and magnetic responses, make the emission one-sided. Toggle the "one-sided" option to compare.

2b. Projecting the wave from a hologram (mode B)

A hologram lit by a reference beam split from the same laser reconstructs any field you recorded or computed, at exactly the laser's frequency. Phase locking is then automatic as long as the two arms differ in length by much less than the coherence length Lc. The partial coherence is modelled as γ = e−ΔL/Lc, which multiplies the interference term. Design the hologram to reconstruct Eholo = A·Et − κ·Einc, where Et is the beam at the Snell angle for index n₂. The sum then equals Et.

There is a theorem that limits this. A field that travels freely in empty space (it satisfies the Helmholtz equation) and is zero throughout any region is zero everywhere. So no projected wave can be zero below a plane and non-zero above it. If Eholo cancels the incident beam above z = 0, it also cancels it below z = 0, all the way back to the hologram. A field that changes abruptly at a surface needs sources on that surface. That is why refraction needs a material (or mode C's sheet) at the interface. Energy follows the same logic. The cancelled incident power is not destroyed: in a real set-up the two arms are combined on a beam splitter, and destructive interference in one output port means constructive interference in the other.

The practical version of the hologram idea is mode C: a thin hologram or metasurface placed at the interface and lit by the incident light itself. It is locked by construction, its sources sit where the bend should start, and it redirects the incident light's own power. Commercial holographic optical elements and metasurface lenses work this way.

3. The one relationship behind all of it

Whatever imposes the extra phase, the transmitted wave has to match the phase of the total field along the sheet. Its tangential wavenumber is the incident one plus the gradient you wrote, plus any multiple of the grating vector:

k·sin θt = k·sin θi + dΦ/dx + m·2π/Λ   ⇔   sin θt = sin θi + (λ/2π)·dΦ/dx + m·λ/Λ
  • Linear ramp (metasurface or phased array), Φ = (Δφ/d)·x: sin θt = sin θi + λΔφ/(2πd). This is the generalized Snell's law of Yu et al. (2011) and the phased-array scan equation.
  • Periodic modulation, Φ = M·sin(2πx/Λ): the grating equation sin θm = sin θi + mλ/Λ. For a thin phase grating (Raman–Nath regime) the power in order m is Jm(M)². A thick grating sends almost everything into one order when the Bragg condition sin θB = λ/(2Λ) holds.
  • Uniform, Φ = const: θt = θi, phase delay δ = arg(1 − κ + A eiφ₀). This is equivalent to a slab with (n−1)Δz = δλ/2π, Feynman's case.
  • A beam no longer exists when |sin θt| > 1. That order becomes evanescent and stays bound to the sheet.

4. Projecting a wave that steers light: the versions that exist

In practice you steer light with another wave by letting that wave write a grating into a medium, and the medium then does the steering.

  • Acousto-optic deflector. A sound wave of frequency f and speed v writes a moving index grating with Λ = v/f. Light leaves at sin θ = sin θi ± λf/v, and the deflection angle is tuned electronically through f. This is a standard laser-scanning component. In the simulation, choose Grating source → Sound wave.
  • Light-written grating (photorefractive crystals, Kerr media, dynamic holography, four-wave mixing). Two pump beams at ±α interfere. Their fringes, with Λ = λp/(2 sin α), change the index, and a probe beam diffracts off the fringes. Here one light wave really does redirect another, with a nonlinear medium as the go-between. In the simulation, choose Grating source → Two pump beams.
  • Active wavefront synthesis (optical phased arrays, SLM holography, metasurfaces) is the phase-locked sheet of section 2 built from engineered sources.

5. How the simulation computes it

2-D scalar wave optics at one frequency (time dependence e−iωt), with lengths in µm. The incident wave is an exact plane wave. Each emitter is a line source with the exact 2-D Green's function (i/4)·H₀(1)(kr), evaluated in its far-field form. Its strength s = −2i·kz·d·c is the value for which a dense sheet radiates exactly the designed field c (one-sided sheets add the cardioid factor (1+cos θ)/2). The far field is the array factor Σ sj e−ikxj sin θ plus the incident beam seen through the same aperture W, normalized so that the incident beam alone peaks at 1. "Measured" angles are the peaks of that computed far field, found independently of the formula, so the agreement in the readout is a real check. Simplifications: scalar field (no polarization), emitters respond as prescribed (no mutual coupling), and the near-field Hankel form is clipped within about 0.1λ of each emitter.
