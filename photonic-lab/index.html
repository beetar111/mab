Light-written photonics · v2 of the light-steering study

Optical circuits written with light

A Fresnel plate (phase hologram) projects pump light onto a photosensitive slab. Wherever the light lands, the slab's refractive index rises (or falls), and that index pattern forms waveguides, interferometers, ring filters and, finally, a 2-bit adder. Every component is simulated twice: once as the light-written version and once as a conventional fabricated silica chip.

What happens on this page, in order

  1. Pattern. You choose what to build. The lab computes the phase pattern for the Fresnel plate (Gerchberg–Saxton algorithm) and the light pattern it actually projects. The projection has speckle, a dim background and a finite sharpness, and all three are measured.
  2. Written index. The projected light becomes a refractive-index map n(x, z) = n₀ + Δn·(pump), blurred by the projector's resolution and the material's diffusion, with the speckle measured in step 1 added on top.
  3. Components. Light at 1550 nm is propagated through that index map with the beam-propagation method: a Mach–Zehnder switch (split into two arms, phase-shift one arm, recombine) and a ring-resonator filter. The same parts are simulated as fabricated silica waveguides for comparison.
  4. Processor. Eleven independently written MZI switches are wired into a 2-bit ripple-carry adder ("directed logic": the input bits set the switches, and the light finds its way to the answer). All 16 input combinations are checked on both platforms.

All 16 inputs on both chips

Does it compute?

1. The idea in one paragraph

In the first simulation, an added wave could steer light only if it was produced at the surface by sources locked to the light. A photosensitive slab does exactly that once it is written: the pump light changes the index, and the index then makes the material's own electrons radiate the right waves (Feynman Ch. 31) wherever a waveguide is wanted. The Fresnel plate is how you tell the slab where. So the circuit is not etched. It is a light pattern, and changing the plate rewrites the processor.

2. The components

  • Waveguide. A strip of raised index. It carries a single mode when V = k₀(w/2)·√(n₁²−n₂²) < π/2. Here w is set to 80% of that limit.
  • Directional coupler. Two guides a gap g apart exchange power as Pcross = sin²(πL/2Lc), where Lc = π/(βeven−βodd) comes from the two "supermodes". The lab solves for both modes, then adjusts the length with BPM until the split is 50:50, including the coupling that happens in the S-bends.
  • Mach–Zehnder switch. Coupler → two arms → coupler. With phase difference Δφ between the arms, Pbar = sin²(Δφ/2) and Pcross = cos²(Δφ/2) for ideal couplers. The needed Δφ = π comes from Δn·L = λ/2: electro-optic Vπ = λd/(n³r₃₃ΓL), thermo-optic ΔTπ = λ/(2L·dn/dT), or extra pump light on that arm. An imperfect coupler (not exactly 50:50) limits the extinction ratio, and calibration cannot fix that.
  • Ring filter. A ring of circumference L = 2πR next to two bus guides. Drop-port transmission: Tdrop = (1−r²)²a / (1 − 2r²a cos φ + r⁴a²), with φ = 2πneffL/λ, self-coupling r and round-trip amplitude a. FSR = λ²/(ngL) and Q = λ/FWHM. The lab gets r from the supermode coupling integrated along the curved gap, C·√(2πR/γ), and gets a from a BPM run in a conformally mapped bend, neq(x) = n(x)(1 + x/R).

3. The 2-bit adder (directed logic)

Each input bit drives MZI switches electrically (or by light). The bits never become light. A continuous laser beam is steered by the switch settings, and the detector it reaches gives the answer. The design uses dual-rail carry: the carry is light on rail "0" or rail "1".

S0 = A0 ⊕ B0: light → SW(A0) → SW(B0), read the swapped rail
C0 = A0·B0: light → SW(A0) → SW(B0); rail "1" only when both cross; the two "0" exits merge in a Y-combiner
S1 = A1 ⊕ B1 ⊕ C0: a copy of the C0 rails → SW(A1) → SW(B1)
C1 (= S2) = generate + propagate·C0: a fresh beam → SW(A1) gives the "kill"/"generate" rail. Two series switches (A1, B1) per rail pass the incoming carry when A1 ⊕ B1 = 1 and the fresh beam otherwise.

That is 11 MZIs, 2 splitters, 1 combiner and 1 crossing. Every switch is a separate BPM simulation with its own random speckle and illumination drift, so the errors that matter (coupler imbalance, phase noise, loss) are physical, not assumed. "Eye opening" is the weakest logical 1 divided by the strongest logical 0 at each detector. Above 0 dB a fixed threshold decodes all 16 inputs correctly.

4. What the simulation does not cover

  • It is 2-D: the slab is treated by the effective-index method in its plane. A real slab also needs vertical confinement (a thin film, or a written depth profile).
  • Paraxial BPM is valid for weak contrast, which suits light-written index (Δn ≈ 10⁻⁴–10⁻²). It cannot model high-contrast silicon or SiN wire waveguides. Those appear only as reference numbers.
  • Photorefractive writing is slower than electronics (ms–s). Reading with 1550 nm light, to which these materials are insensitive, keeps the pattern intact. Free-carrier writing in silicon is fast (ns) but adds absorption, which the lab includes using the Soref–Bennett relations.
  • Crossing crosstalk, back-reflections and polarization are ignored. The hologram is computed on a 256×256 tile. A real chip needs millions of plate pixels or stitched exposures, and the budget table shows how many.
